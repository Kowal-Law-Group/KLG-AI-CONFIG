# KLG Briefing Standards

This reference file contains the brief-specific writing and
citation standards that apply when drafting or revising brief
language. It draws from the Briefing Assistant prompt in the
KLG AI OS and supplements the project-level style guide and
claude.md with brief-specific conventions.

Note: This file is a quick-reference for the brief elevation
skill. The authoritative sources are `/mnt/project/claude.md`
and `/mnt/project/klg-style-guide.md`. If anything here
conflicts, those files control.

## Brief Argument Structure

This is a general rubric, not a rigid formula. Apply it
flexibly based on the argument.

### 1. Roadmap Paragraph
Open with a concise paragraph previewing the issue, the
controlling rule, and the bottom-line conclusion. The reader
should know where the argument is going before it begins.

### 2. Rule Section
State the governing rule with complete in-text citations to
controlling authority. In California briefs, place all fact
and legal citations inside parentheses as separate citation
sentences following the substantive sentence.

### 3. Application
Apply the rule to the facts in discrete paragraphs, one main
point per paragraph, each supported by citations. Connect
each fact to the rule element it satisfies.

### 4. Error Identification (Appellant Posture)
When arguing error: explicitly identify the trial court's
error and explain prejudice — why it mattered and how it
harmed our client. Apply the correct prejudice test (Watson
reasonable probability / Chapman beyond reasonable doubt /
structural error).

### 5. Conclusion
End with a short summing-up paragraph stating the requested
disposition.

## Introduction Standards (Funnel Approach)

### Opening Briefs
- First line: what order we are appealing
- First paragraph: snapshot of case, how it ended, why it
  was wrong
- Then: short paragraphs for each core argument
- Overall: Tell them what you're going to tell them. Then
  tell them. Then tell them what you told them.

### Reply Briefs
All reply arguments should follow:
1. In the AOB, we argue x.
2. In the RB, respondents reject x by arguing y.
3. But y is wrong because z.

Focus on the RB. Never sound like new arguments.

## Citation Standards (Brief-Specific)

### Citation Placement
- End the substantive sentence with normal punctuation
- Add a separate citation sentence immediately after
- Citation sentence has its own terminal punctuation inside
  the closing parenthesis
- Do NOT embed citations within the substantive sentence

### Record Citations
- REF format: (REF251021-00001.)
- Appendix: (1-AA-1.) — no spaces in "1AA"
- Clerk's Transcript: (1-CT-1.)
- Reporter's Transcript: (RT [page]:[line]) or
  (Vol. [X] RT [page]:[line])
- Short forms use p. or pp. before pincite pages

### Case Citations
- California Style Manual for CA authorities:
  Party v. Party (Year) Volume Cal.5th Page, Pinpoint
- Bluebook for federal authorities:
  Party v. Party, Volume F.3d Page, Pinpoint (9th Cir. Year)
- No superscript in reporters or courts
- Always include pincites for quoted or closely paraphrased
  propositions
- Include explanatory parentheticals (no naked cites)
- Italicize case names only, not the rest of the citation

### Unpublished Cases
- Use ONLY if directly on point and no published authority
  addresses the issue
- Flag conspicuously: [UNPUBLISHED]

## Heading Standards

- Every page should have a heading (rule of thumb)
- No more than a full page of text without a heading
- Headings must be complete sentences with punctuation and
  active verbs — they persuade, not just label
- Top-level headings: Small Caps (type in Title Case, apply
  Small Caps formatting)
- SOF/SOC headings: begin with dates (month and year)
- SOC subheadings (P3-level or italic narrative subheadings)
  must also be complete sentences — not fragments. A reader
  scanning the headings alone should understand the story.
  Example: "The trial court enters the identical order as
  permanent." not "Permanent injunction."
- Test: reading only the headings in the TOC, the reader
  should get a good sense of the case and issues

## Court Name Conventions

- **"Superior Court"** — Always capitalized when used as the
  court's formal name (e.g., "the Superior Court entered a
  preliminary injunction"). This is a proper noun referring to
  a specific court.
- **"trial court"** — Always lowercase when used as a generic
  descriptor (e.g., "the trial court erred"). This is an
  informal shorthand, not a proper name.
- Never use lowercase "superior court." If you mean the formal
  name, capitalize it. If you mean the generic reference, use
  "trial court" instead.

## Rules of Court Citation Format

In California state court filings, use the short form:

- **Correct:** Rules of Ct., rule 8.1008(a)
- **Incorrect:** Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.1008(a)
- **Incorrect:** California Rules of Court, rule 8.1008(a)

The abbreviated form ("Rules of Ct.") is standard in California
appellate practice and saves words without sacrificing clarity.

## Block Quote Formatting

Block quotes use indentation — not quotation marks — to signal
that the text is a direct quotation. The formatting rules:

1. **Style:** Use the Quote style (or BodyText with left/right
   indentation). Do NOT wrap block-quoted text in quotation
   marks — the indentation itself communicates that the text is
   quoted. Adding quotation marks is redundant.
2. **Citation paragraph:** The citation following a block quote
   uses BodyTextContinued style (no first-line indent), placed
   immediately after the block quote as a separate paragraph.
   This visually connects the citation to the quoted material
   without the awkward first-line indent that BodyText would add.
3. **When to block-quote:** Only block-quote language from
   statutes, court opinions establishing the governing rule, or
   contractual provisions — and only when the exact wording
   matters. Never block-quote to discuss cases (summarize and
   cite instead). Judges dislike block quotes; use them sparingly
   and only when the precise words do work that paraphrase cannot.

## Prayer and Conclusion Conventions

Avoid fusty, archaic prayer language. Specifically:

- Do NOT use "WHEREFORE" to introduce the prayer for relief.
  Simply state what the petitioner (or appellant) requests.
- Do NOT use "respectfully prays" or "respectfully submits"
  as filler. Just make the request directly.
- Do NOT use other archaic legal terms: "Comes now," "prays
  this Honorable Court," "for good cause shown," etc.

The prayer should read like a direct, confident request — not
a medieval petition to the sovereign.

## Prohibited Patterns

### Words and Phrases to Avoid
- "instant case"
- "furthermore," "therefore," "therefrom"
- "it is axiomatic," "clearly," "it is well established that"
- "respectfully" as filler
- "as such" (use "So" instead)
- "hereinabove," "hereinbelow," "aforementioned"
- "i.e." (use "namely" or "for example")
- "Moreover" (signals only "I'm still talking")
- "However" at the start of a sentence (use "But")
- ALL CAPS for anything
- "WHEREFORE" and other fusty prayer/conclusion language

### Structural Patterns to Avoid
- Starting a sentence with "In Smith v. Jones..." before
  explaining why the reader should care about Smith v. Jones
- Block quoting to discuss cases (judges hate this)
- Long paragraphs without breaks
- Multiple forms of emphasis (bold + italics)
- Footnotes (use sparingly; consider parenthetical instead)
- Unnecessary definitions and acronyms

## Prose Quality Standards

- Write like a normal person would talk
- Short sentences, front-loaded points, vigorous verbs
- Prefer "because" over "as" or "since" for causal
- Active voice by default
- Start sentences with punchy, descriptive words
- Err toward shorter paragraphs (if you wonder whether a
  point needs a new paragraph, it does)
- Chekhov's Gun: if you introduce it, it must matter
- Say as much as you need to say to be persuasive. Then stop.

## Standard of Review Integration

When reviewing or revising brief language, ensure that:

- The standard of review is explicitly stated for each issue
- The analysis matches the standard (e.g., substantial
  evidence review focuses on the record, not credibility;
  de novo review focuses on legal principles)
- The brief does not make arguments inconsistent with the
  applicable standard

Standards (for reference):
- De novo: questions of law, statutory interpretation
- Substantial evidence: factual findings
- Abuse of discretion: discretionary rulings, evidentiary,
  sanctions
- Clear error: findings of fact in bench trials (federal)
