# Elite Appellate Panel Review Framework

This reference file contains the detailed review criteria and
deliverable structure for the strategic brief review. It draws
from the Elite Appellate Panel Brief Review and the NotebookLM
Brief Critique prompts in the KLG AI OS.

## The Panel

The review is conducted from the combined perspectives of three
elite appellate practitioners:

- **Bryan Garner** — Clarity, structure, and prose discipline.
  Focus: Is every sentence earning its place? Is the brief
  organized for maximum readability? Is the prose clean, direct,
  and free of clutter?

- **Paul Clement** — Strategic framing, issue selection, and
  institutional credibility. Focus: Are the right issues raised
  at the right level of generality? Does the brief position the
  court to want to rule in our favor? Does it address
  administrability and precedent implications?

- **Lisa Blatt** — Advocacy force, narrative drive, and judicial
  persuasion. Focus: Is there a compelling story? Does the brief
  immediately signal why the court should care? Is the tone
  confident without being aggressive?

## Review Criteria

### 1. Narrative and Framing

- Is there a clear, compelling story of the case?
- Does the brief immediately signal why the court should care?
- Are the equities, stakes, and institutional concerns framed
  effectively for an appellate audience?
- Does the introduction follow the KLG funnel approach?
  1. What is the dispute about? (lay terms)
  2. What is the case about? (legal and factual theories)
  3. What is the appeal about? (key issues remaining)
- For opening briefs: Does the first line tell the reader what
  order we are appealing? Does the first paragraph give a
  snapshot of the case, how it ended, and why it was wrong?
- For reply briefs: Does each argument follow the x/y/z format?
  (In the AOB, we argue x. In the RB, respondents argue y.
  But y is wrong because z.)
- Identify missed opportunities to sharpen the narrative or
  reframe the dispute at a higher level.

### 2. Issue Selection and Presentation

- Are the right issues being raised?
- Are issues framed at the optimal level of generality?
  (Too specific = the court can distinguish; too general =
  the court has no reason to reach your case.)
- Are any issues unnecessary, dilutive, or counterproductive?
- Is the strongest issue presented first (or is there a
  strategic reason for a different order)?
- Are headings persuasive sentences with active verbs and
  terminal punctuation? Upon reading only the headings in
  the table of contents, does the reader get a good sense
  of the case and issues?
- Recommend consolidation, reordering, or reframing where
  appropriate.

### 3. Logical Structure and Flow

- Does the brief proceed in a clear, inevitable, and
  disciplined progression?
- Are there structural redundancies, detours, or sequencing
  problems?
- Does each section build on what came before?
- Are transitions between sections clear and logical?
- Is the rule section complete before the application section
  begins? (No interleaving rule and application.)
- Identify where the argument can be simplified, tightened,
  or made more linear.

### 4. Argument Development

- Are core arguments presented crisply and driven to their
  strongest conclusions?
- Does each argument section follow the KLG structure?
  1. Roadmap paragraph (issue, controlling rule, conclusion)
  2. Rule section (rule + controlling authorities)
  3. Application (one point per paragraph, with cites)
  4. Error identification and prejudice (if arguing error)
  5. Conclusion (short, stating requested disposition)
- Are there places where points are underdeveloped,
  overexplained, or repeated?
- Is the best authority being used for each proposition?
  Are there stronger authorities available?
- Are contrary authorities addressed? (At least one per
  major contention.)
- Is prejudice clearly established for every claimed error?
- Identify where doctrinal analysis, policy, or practical
  consequences should be amplified or restrained.

### 5. Tone, Voice, and Judicial Credibility

- Does the brief sound confident, authoritative, and restrained?
- Are there places where the tone is defensive, overstated, or
  argumentative in a way that could alienate judges?
- Does the brief avoid legalese and banned phrases (per KLG
  Style Guide)?
- Is the brief written in plain English that a busy appellate
  justice can absorb quickly?
- Does the brief help the court? (Most briefs are an ocean of
  irrelevance — is this one different?)
- Recommend adjustments to enhance credibility and trust.

### 6. Statement of Facts / Statement of the Case

- Is the SOF a narrative, not a document dump?
- Does it lead with the facts that favor our position?
- Are facts properly separated from procedural history?
  (SOF = events before complaint; SOC = events after complaint)
- Does every factual assertion cite to the record?
- Are unfavorable facts acknowledged rather than hidden?
  (Hiding them destroys credibility.)
- Is the SOF chronological, thematic, or some effective
  combination?
- Does the SOF set up the legal arguments that follow?

### 7. Introduction and Conclusion

- Does the introduction create a clear roadmap?
- Does it present the case narrative compellingly?
- Does it show why the client wins on the law?
- Does it show why the equities support the client?
- Does it demonstrate trial court error AND prejudice?
- Is the conclusion more than boilerplate? Does it leave
  the reader with the single most compelling reason to rule
  in our favor?

## Deliverable Structure

### A. Executive Assessment

A concise, candid assessment of the brief at a strategic level.
This section should:

- Identify top 3 strengths (with short notes on why they work)
- Identify top 3 weaknesses (with concrete ways to fix them)
- Name the single most important improvement that would most
  increase the brief's effectiveness
- Assess the brief's current "ceiling" — how good could this
  brief be with the existing record and authorities?

Cap at ~500 words.

### B. High-Leverage Recommendations

A prioritized list of specific, high-impact recommendations.
For each:

- **What to change** — specific and concrete
- **Why it matters** — how it improves appellate persuasion
- **How to implement it** — enough detail for the attorney
  to act on it or for Claude to draft the revision
- **Effort level** — Quick fix / Moderate rewrite / Major
  restructuring

Limit to 7–10 recommendations. More than that dilutes focus.

### C. Punch List of Proposed Changes

Concrete, paste-ready proposed revisions. For each:

- **Location** — Section, paragraph, approximate page
  (e.g., "Introduction, ¶2," "Argument I.A opening paragraph")
- **Current text** — Quote enough to locate the passage
- **Proposed revision** — Complete replacement paragraph(s)
  with citations
- **Rationale** — One sentence on why this version is stronger

Focus on changes that materially improve framing, flow, or
force — not cosmetic edits.

### D. Master Framing Paragraph

A proposed "master framing paragraph" that ties the best
arguments into a single persuasive narrative suitable for the
introduction or conclusion. This is the one paragraph that,
if the reader remembers nothing else, captures why we win.

## Add-On Review Modes

These can be appended to any review path:

### Aggressive Trimming
"Assume the brief must be cut by 20%. Identify what should
be deleted or consolidated first."

Prioritize cuts by what loses the least persuasive value.
Work from weakest to strongest. Identify passages that are
repetitive, tangential, or making points the court doesn't
need. Flag block quotes that can be replaced with integrated
quotation.

### Hostile Panel
"Assume a skeptical or hostile panel. Identify vulnerabilities
and recommend preemptive reframing."

For each vulnerability:
- What is the hostile question from the bench?
- Where does the brief currently fail to answer it?
- How should the brief be revised to preempt it?

### Institutional Audience
"Assume the court is especially concerned with administrability,
precedent, and institutional consequences."

Evaluate whether the brief addresses:
- How the requested ruling would work in practice
- Whether it creates a clear, administrable rule
- How it fits with existing precedent
- Whether it opens the door to unintended consequences

### Section-Only Review
Limit the review to specified sections. Apply the same
criteria but at greater depth for the targeted sections.

## Comparative Evaluation

When opposing briefs are available, also evaluate:

- Where are we strongest relative to them?
- Where do they have the advantage?
- What "holes" will the other side exploit?
- What defenses or reframes should we prepare?
- What concessions did they make that we should exploit?
- What arguments did they conspicuously avoid? Why?
