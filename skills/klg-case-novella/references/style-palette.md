# Style Palette — Author Voice Profiles

This reference provides detailed voice profiles for narrative
drafting. The skill presents a simplified menu; this file
contains the full guidance Claude needs during drafting.

---

## Voice Selection Rules

1. **One primary voice, one optional secondary.** More than
   two influences cancel each other out. The primary voice
   controls sentence structure, pacing, and POV technique.
   The secondary voice contributes specific techniques
   (e.g., Gladwell's explanatory turns inside a Wolfe-style
   narrative).

2. **Ask every session.** After presenting the default palette,
   always ask: "Is there a specific author or influence you
   have in mind? We can use anyone — the defaults are just
   starting points."

3. **If the user names a custom author,** Claude should
   identify the author's signature techniques and integrate
   them the same way the defaults are structured below.
   Research via web search if needed.

4. **Voice consistency across chapters.** Once locked in
   Phase B, the voice must not drift. The continuity log
   between chapter batches should include a voice-consistency
   check.

## Default Profiles

### 1. Tom Wolfe — New Journalism

**When to recommend:** Law/policy cases, institutional
behavior cases, cases where you need to feel the system
operating on people. Default recommendation for most cases.

**Signature techniques:**
- Immersive scene-setting with status-conscious detail
  (what people wear, drive, eat — as social signifiers)
- Interior monologue rendered in third person with the
  character's own vocabulary
- Onomatopoeia and typographic percussion (exclamation
  points, ellipses, em dashes, italicized inner voice)
- Institutional satire through accumulated specifics —
  never editorializing, just showing the machinery
- Long sentences that build momentum, short sentences
  that punch
- POV that zooms from wide-angle sociological observation
  to tight character interiority

**Drafting constraints:**
- Do not overuse exclamation points — they are percussion,
  not decoration. Two or three per chapter maximum.
- Onomatopoeia should be grounded in the scene (a phone
  chime, a courtroom door, a keyboard) — not abstract.
- Status details must serve characterization, not padding.
- The satire must be observational, never preachy.

**Example register:**
"The phone was in the cradle like a sacred object,
perfectly centered above the air vents. Eddie had
adjusted the mount so the phone didn't wobble when
the car hit the little ripples in the asphalt, those
waves of ancient oil spills and tire marks that made
every parking lot look like a dirty lake from above."


### 2. Malcolm Gladwell — Explanatory Narrative

**When to recommend:** Cases with a non-obvious legal theory,
cases where you need the reader to see the issue differently
than conventional wisdom suggests.

**Signature techniques:**
- The "counterintuitive turn" — set up what everyone
  believes, then reveal why it's wrong or incomplete
- Clean, accessible prose — short paragraphs, no jargon
  without immediate explanation
- Anecdote-first structure: open with a specific story,
  zoom out to the principle, return to the story
- Named social-science concepts used as framing devices
- Confident, slightly professorial narrator voice
- Questions posed directly to the reader

**Drafting constraints:**
- Do not turn every legal issue into a Gladwell "insight."
  Use the counterintuitive turn sparingly — once or twice
  per chapter, not every paragraph.
- Avoid the Gladwell tendency to oversimplify. Legal
  nuance must survive the explanatory treatment.
- The narrator should explain, not lecture.

**Example register:**
"Here is something strange about the way California
regulates rideshare companies. The Public Utilities
Commission calls them 'transportation network companies'
— a phrase that contains the word 'transportation.' The
insurance they're required to carry is transportation
insurance. The permits they hold are transportation
permits. And yet, when someone is injured in one of
their vehicles, these companies argue they are not in
the transportation business."


### 3. Joseph Epstein — Urbane Essayist

**When to recommend:** Cases with sophisticated parties,
business disputes, trust litigation, professional
malpractice — where the drama is in restraint and
implication rather than spectacle.

**Signature techniques:**
- Understated irony — the humor is in what's left unsaid
- Cultured allusions (literature, history, social manners)
  deployed without pretension
- Short, declarative sentences that carry enormous weight
- Characters revealed through their habits, preferences,
  and small decisions rather than dramatic confrontation
- Essayistic asides from the narrator that illuminate
  without interrupting
- Dry wit as the default register

**Drafting constraints:**
- Do not mistake urbanity for detachment. The prose must
  still serve the case — every digression earns its place.
- Avoid snobbery. Epstein's voice is sophisticated but
  never condescending toward his subjects.
- Keep allusions accessible — a reference to Trollope is
  fine if it illuminates; gratuitous erudition is not.

**Example register:**
"The trustee had the look of a man who believed that
competence was its own reward, and who had recently
discovered — with some irritation — that it was not."


### 4. Ken Follett — Historical Sweep

**When to recommend:** Complex multi-party cases, cases with
a long procedural history, cases where the timeline itself
is the architecture.

**Signature techniques:**
- Multiple POV threads that converge on a single event
  or decision
- Architectural pacing — each thread advances per chapter,
  building toward an inevitable collision
- Rich period detail (or, in legal context, institutional
  and procedural detail that grounds the reader in place)
- Characters defined by the historical forces acting on
  them as much as by personal choice
- Clean, propulsive prose — readable above all

**Drafting constraints:**
- Do not let the sweep overwhelm the legal substance.
  Each POV thread must advance a legal theme.
- Keep the timeline clear — readers must always know
  where they are chronologically.
- Avoid the Follett tendency toward melodrama. The legal
  stakes provide sufficient drama.

**Example register:**
"Three things happened in Sacramento that October, and
none of the people involved knew about the others. In
a hearing room on the fourth floor of the PUC building,
a staff attorney drafted a new insurance requirement.
In a campaign office two blocks away, a coalition
strategist finalized ballot language. And in a hospital
in Fresno, a woman whose name would appear in none of
these documents tried to explain to an adjuster why her
shoulder would never work the same way again."


### 5. John Grisham — Legal Procedural

**When to recommend:** Fact-intensive cases, cases where
trial-level events drive the appeal, cases where you need
to internalize the record chronology.

**Signature techniques:**
- Tight procedural pacing — legal mechanics rendered as
  suspense
- Lawyer-protagonist POV with internal strategic monologue
- Courtroom scenes as set pieces — precise procedural
  detail made dramatic
- Short chapters that end on pivots
- Accessible prose — never showing off, always moving

**Drafting constraints:**
- Do not reduce the legal analysis to thriller mechanics.
  The suspense should emerge from genuine legal stakes,
  not manufactured cliffhangers.
- Avoid the Grisham tendency toward conspiracy or
  corruption plots. The novella must stay grounded in
  the actual case.
- Courtroom scenes must be procedurally accurate.

**Example register:**
"Harmon opened his laptop and pulled up the motion.
Twelve pages. Six cases cited. One statute. The whole
argument turned on a single word in Section 7451:
'between.' If 'between' meant what his client said it
meant, the jury would never hear the word 'employee.'
If it meant what the plaintiff's lawyer said it meant,
his client was looking at a seven-figure verdict."


### 6. Michael Lewis — Contrarian Character Study

**When to recommend:** Cases where one party's position is
counterintuitive but correct, whistleblower cases, cases
where the standard narrative is wrong.

**Signature techniques:**
- Character-first structure — find the person who saw
  what no one else saw, and tell the story through them
- The "smart outsider" protagonist archetype
- Institutional dysfunction revealed through individual
  experience
- Humor as diagnostic tool — funny moments that expose
  systemic absurdity
- Data and specifics deployed for narrative impact

**Drafting constraints:**
- The "contrarian insight" structure only works if the
  insight is genuine. Do not force a Michael Lewis frame
  onto a case that doesn't have a hidden-truth structure.
- Avoid hagiography — even the protagonist should have
  blind spots.
- Keep the humor earned, not imposed.

**Example register:**
"The thing about Kim Hwang was that she didn't care
about Proposition 22. She didn't care about 'gig
economy workers' or 'independent contractor status'
or any of the phrases that made newspaper editors
feel important. What Kim cared about was the policy
number on the screen in front of her, which told her
that Uber Technologies, Inc. carried commercial auto
liability insurance. If you carried commercial auto
liability insurance, you were in the transportation
business. It was, she thought, not a difficult
concept."


### 7. Erik Larson — Dual Narrative

**When to recommend:** Cases with two distinct storylines
that collide (e.g., a regulatory proceeding running parallel
to the underlying tort), cases with dramatic irony.

**Signature techniques:**
- Parallel timelines — two (or more) narrative threads
  that the reader knows will converge
- Dramatic irony as the dominant mode — the reader
  understands the collision before the characters do
- Meticulous period/institutional detail
- Measured, almost stately pacing that builds dread or
  inevitability
- Minimal editorializing — the structure does the work

**Drafting constraints:**
- The dual-narrative structure requires clear signposting.
  The reader must always know which thread they're in.
- Do not stretch the parallel structure beyond what the
  case supports. Two genuine threads, not a forced duality.
- The convergence point must be earned — it should feel
  inevitable in retrospect.

**Example register:**
"On the morning of October 14, while the CACI committee
met in a conference room in downtown Los Angeles to
discuss proposed revisions to the jury instruction on
'peculiar risk,' a driver named Eddie Santos accepted
a trip request on an app that had been downloaded
4.2 million times in California alone. Neither event,
by itself, would have mattered."


### 8. Agatha Christie — Puzzle Architecture

**When to recommend:** Cases where the legal outcome turns
on how evidence fits together, credibility disputes where
the reader needs to weigh competing narratives, cases with
a "who knew what when" structure.

**Signature techniques:**
- Clues planted early and revisited with new significance
- Multiple suspects/explanations that are systematically
  narrowed — the reader participates in the deduction
- Clean, efficient prose — every scene serves the puzzle
- Characters revealed through what they conceal
- The satisfying "reveal" where scattered evidence clicks
  into a single coherent picture
- Misdirection as structural device — the obvious answer
  is never the right one

**Drafting constraints:**
- The puzzle structure must be honest — no withholding
  information the reader needs. Plant all clues fairly.
- Do not reduce legal analysis to a whodunit. The "puzzle"
  is which legal framework applies, or which version of
  events is credible, not a mystery novel gimmick.
- The reveal must correspond to a genuine legal insight,
  not a narrative trick.

**Example register:**
"The deposition transcript said one thing. The email
chain said another. And the phone records — which
neither party had wanted to discuss in detail — said
something else entirely. It was the phone records, of
course, that mattered. They always do. The question
was not whether the call happened, but why both sides
had agreed, without ever discussing it, to pretend
it hadn't."


### 9. James Michener — Epic Scope

**When to recommend:** Cases with deep historical or
regulatory context, cases where understanding the origin
and evolution of a legal framework is essential to
understanding the current dispute, cases spanning decades
of institutional development.

**Signature techniques:**
- Begin far before the immediate dispute — establish the
  historical foundation
- Layer-by-layer construction from geological/historical
  context to present-day characters
- Multiple generations or institutional eras connected
  by a single thread (a statute, a property, a legal
  principle)
- Encyclopedic detail that makes the reader an expert
  in the subject by immersion
- Patient, cumulative pacing — the scope IS the point

**Drafting constraints:**
- The historical sweep must earn its place. Every era or
  layer must connect to the present-day legal issue.
  Do not include background for its own sake.
- Keep the present-day characters grounded and specific
  even as the scope expands.
- Monitor pacing — Michener's technique risks losing
  urgency. Use the present-day stakes as an anchor to
  return to periodically.

**Example register:**
"The statute that would decide Judy Peck's case was
born in 1872, in a California that did not yet have
paved roads in most of its cities. The legislature
that year was concerned with a problem that seems
quaint now: what happens when a man hires a carriage,
and the driver — not his employee, but an independent
operator — runs someone down? The answer they wrote
into the Civil Code would survive, essentially
unchanged, for a hundred and fifty years. And it
would land, with all its nineteenth-century assumptions
intact, on a twenty-first-century judge's desk."


### 10. Albert Camus — Existential Clarity

**When to recommend:** Cases with stark moral dimensions,
cases where institutional absurdity is the core problem,
cases where a party is caught in a system that operates
by its own logic regardless of human consequences. Also
useful for cases involving bureaucratic indifference or
procedural traps.

**Signature techniques:**
- Spare, direct prose — no ornamentation
- Moral weight carried by what characters observe rather
  than what they feel
- Institutional absurdity rendered without satire — simply
  described, which makes it more devastating
- The outsider's perspective: a character who sees the
  system clearly because they are not part of it
- Short declarative sentences with enormous implication
- Silence and absence as narrative tools

**Drafting constraints:**
- Do not confuse existential tone with nihilism. The story
  must still serve the case — the reader should emerge
  with sharper conviction, not despair.
- Avoid the French philosophical novel tendency toward
  abstraction. Keep scenes concrete and grounded in the
  case materials.
- The spare prose must still convey legal nuance. Do not
  sacrifice analytical precision for atmosphere.

**Example register:**
"The hearing was scheduled for nine. He arrived at
eight-thirty and sat in the hallway. The hallway was
long and empty and smelled of floor wax. At nine-fifteen
a clerk opened the door and called a name that was not
his. At nine-forty-five the clerk opened the door again.
This time it was his name. He stood and walked in. The
judge did not look up."


### 11. George Orwell — Political Precision

**When to recommend:** Cases where language is being used to
obscure reality, cases involving regulatory doublespeak or
statutory construction where the words have been stretched
beyond their natural meaning, cases where institutional
power operates through the control of categories and
definitions.

**Signature techniques:**
- Crystal-clear prose — the enemy of obfuscation
- Direct exposure of how institutional language distorts
  reality ("independent contractor" means "worker we
  control but do not wish to be responsible for")
- The essay-within-the-narrative: brief, devastating
  passages where the narrator cuts through jargon
- Concrete, sensory detail deployed to contrast with
  abstract institutional claims
- Moral seriousness without sentimentality
- The recurring motif: what a thing is called vs. what
  a thing is

**Drafting constraints:**
- Orwell's clarity is a tool, not a weapon. The prose
  should expose, not prosecute. Let the reader draw
  the conclusion.
- Do not turn the narrative into a polemic. Orwell at
  his best is a reporter first and a moralist second.
- Resist the temptation to be preachy. Orwell's power
  comes from showing, not telling.

**Example register:**
"Uber called Eddie an 'independent contractor-driver.'
The phrase had the quality of all institutional
language: it described a relationship in terms that
made the relationship invisible. An independent
contractor is someone who chooses. A driver is someone
who drives. Put them together and you have a person
who freely chooses to drive, which sounds like a
hobby. What you do not have, in the phrase, is a
company that tells a person where to go, how fast to
get there, and what will happen to his income if he
declines."


### 12. Larry McMurtry — Western Realism

**When to recommend:** Cases with a strong sense of place
(particularly the American West or rural settings), cases
involving land use, property disputes, water rights, or
resource conflicts, cases with pragmatic, unsentimental
parties, and cases where the landscape or geography
matters to the story.

**Signature techniques:**
- Landscape as character — the physical setting shapes
  behavior and outcomes
- Pragmatic, unsentimental voice — characters described
  by what they do, not what they feel
- Dialogue that reveals character through rhythm and
  vocabulary rather than exposition
- The long view: characters who have seen things come
  and go, who understand that institutions outlast people
- Quiet humor — dry, observational, never broad
- An unflinching directness about consequences

**Drafting constraints:**
- The Western realism tone requires genuine grounding in
  place. Do not force a McMurtry voice onto a case that
  has no sense of landscape or geography.
- Avoid romanticizing rural or working-class characters.
  McMurtry's power is his refusal to romanticize.
- Keep the legal analysis integrated into character
  perspective — these are practical people who understand
  law as a fact of life, not an abstraction.

**Example register:**
"The property had been in the family since 1947, which
in California real estate meant it had been in the
family forever. The trust document said one thing. The
family understood another. The lawyer explained that
what the family understood did not matter, legally, and
the family explained that what the lawyer understood
did not matter, practically, and the judge would
eventually have to decide which kind of not-mattering
was the kind the law cared about."

## Blending Rules

When the user selects a primary + secondary voice:

1. **Structure and pacing** come from the primary.
2. **Specific techniques** can be borrowed from the secondary.
   Examples:
   - "Wolfe primary, Gladwell secondary" = Immersive
     scene-setting (Wolfe) with occasional explanatory
     turns where the narrator zooms out to explain a
     legal concept before zooming back in (Gladwell).
   - "Follett primary, Epstein secondary" = Multi-POV
     sweep (Follett) with dry, understated character
     observations (Epstein).
   - "Grisham primary, Lewis secondary" = Procedural
     pacing (Grisham) with a contrarian protagonist
     who sees what others miss (Lewis).

3. **If techniques conflict,** the primary wins. For
   example, Wolfe's maximalist detail conflicts with
   Epstein's minimalism — if Wolfe is primary, keep the
   detail; if Epstein is primary, keep the restraint.

4. **Voice-consistency check:** At the end of each chapter
   batch, confirm: "Does this still sound like [primary]
   with [secondary] influence? Or has one voice taken over?"
   Adjust in the next batch if needed.

## Custom Author Integration

If the user names an author not on the default list:

1. Identify the author's 5–6 signature techniques using
   Claude's knowledge (supplement with web search if the
   author is less well-known).
2. Write a brief profile in the same format as the defaults
   above.
3. Present it to the user for confirmation before proceeding.
4. Apply the same blending rules.

Common custom requests to anticipate:
- **Cormac McCarthy** — sparse, biblical, no quotation marks.
  Useful for cases with stark moral dimensions.
- **Joan Didion** — precise, cool, structurally experimental.
  Useful for California institutional critique.
- **Ta-Nehisi Coates** — direct address, personal stakes,
  systemic analysis rendered as lived experience.
  Useful for civil rights cases.
- **Michael Crichton** — technical detail as thriller fuel,
  expert-protagonist structure. Useful for cases involving
  complex technical or scientific evidence.
