# Argument Map Framework

This reference defines the structure for the Phase B Strategic
Outline & Argument Map. Each section should be populated with
case-specific content, authorities, and record citations.

---

## Section 1: Case Posture & Relief

- **Single-sentence "why we win":** One sentence that captures
  the core reason the court should rule in our favor. This is
  the sentence the attorney should be able to say if the
  presiding justice asks "What is this case about?"

- **Relief sought:** Specific, concrete statement of what we
  are asking the court to do. Not "reversal" in the abstract —
  the exact disposition: reverse and remand with directions to
  [X], vacate the order and direct the trial court to [Y],
  issue a writ of mandate directing [Z]. The attorney must be
  able to articulate this crisply at any moment during argument.

- **Procedural posture:** One-line description — appeal from
  what type of order/judgment, what happened below, how it
  got here. Include standard(s) of review for each issue.

---

## Section 2: Argument Inventory (Offense)

Map our strongest position across four dimensions. For each,
include the top 2–3 arguments with supporting authorities.

### Our Strongest Legal Arguments
- What are the strongest legal grounds for reversal/relief?
- What controlling authorities support us?
- Where is the standard of review most favorable?
- Include pincites to key holdings.

### Our Strongest Equitable Arguments
- What fairness considerations favor our client?
- Reliance interests, disproportionate impact, procedural
  unfairness, sympathetic facts
- Are there institutional or systemic concerns that support
  our position (access to justice, administrability, etc.)?

### Our Most Compelling Remedies
- What is the cleanest remedy to ask for?
- Is it narrow enough to be palatable to the court?
- Does it avoid collateral consequences the court might
  worry about?
- Is there a fallback remedy if the court is reluctant to
  grant the primary ask?

### Our Strongest Theme / Narrative
- What is the overarching story of the case from our
  perspective?
- What is the one-sentence framing that makes the court
  want to rule for us?
- How does this theme connect the legal arguments to the
  equities to the remedy?
- Is this theme already developed in the briefing, or is
  it new for oral argument?

---

## Section 3: Vulnerability Inventory (Defense)

The mirror image. For each dimension, identify the other
side's strongest position and our weakest points.

### Our Biggest Legal Weakness
- What is the strongest legal argument against us?
- What adverse authority is most on point?
- Where does the standard of review hurt us most?
- What is our best response to this weakness?

### Their Strongest Equities
- What sympathetic facts favor the other side?
- Are there reliance interests, institutional concerns, or
  policy considerations that cut against us?
- What will the court find most troubling about ruling for us?

### Our Remedy Problems
- Is the remedy we're asking for overbroad? Unworkable?
- Does it create collateral consequences the court might worry
  about (floodgates, administrability, perverse incentives)?
- If we win, what does remand look like — is there a risk the
  same result occurs on remand?
- Are there scope-of-relief issues (e.g., we asked for more
  than the record supports)?

### Their Strongest Theme / Narrative
- What is the overarching story from the other side's
  perspective?
- What is the one-sentence framing that makes the court
  want to rule against us?
- How would a skeptical judge summarize the case in a way
  that favors the other side?
- What is the best way to disrupt or reframe their narrative?

---

## Section 4: Best and Worst Case Scenarios

### Best Case
- What does a full win look like — the ideal disposition?
- What is the cleanest path to get there?
- Which arguments, if accepted, produce this result?

### Worst Case
- What does a full loss look like?
- What is the damage to the client?
- Is there a partial-loss scenario that is survivable?
- What would a loss opinion say — what reasoning would the
  court use?

### Most Likely Outcome
- Realistic calibration based on the strength of arguments,
  panel tendencies, procedural posture, and briefing quality
- Is a partial win likely? Which issues does the court seem
  most receptive to?
- Any signals from the briefing or panel composition?

---

## Section 5: Theme Analysis

### Themes from Our Briefs
- Identify narrative threads, recurring framings, and
  rhetorical motifs already developed in the briefing
- Note how often each theme appears and where it is strongest

### Proposed Additional Themes
- Themes Claude identifies from the argument map, panel
  intel, or policy implications that were not developed
  in the briefing but could be effective at oral argument

### User-Identified Themes
- Themes the user wants to stress-test (gathered during
  Phase B, Step B.2)

### Theme Stress-Test
- For each theme: When does it help us? When does it risk
  overreach? What will the other side say in response?
  How should we cabin it?

---

## Section 6: Procedural Gauntlet

This section pressure-tests every threshold issue the
panel might raise — either sua sponte or at the prompting
of opposing counsel. The goal is to ensure the attorney
is not caught flat-footed on any procedural question.

### Appealability
- Is the order/judgment appealable as a matter of right?
- Any one-final-judgment problems (CCP § 904.1)?
- Collateral order doctrine issues?
- Death knell doctrine applicability?
- If a writ: is this an appropriate case for extraordinary
  relief? Have we met the Omaha Indemnity factors?

### Timeliness
- When was the appealable order/judgment entered?
- When was notice of entry served (Cal. Rules of Court,
  rule 8.104)?
- When was the NOA filed?
- Was it timely under rule 8.104(a)(1) (60 days from
  notice of entry) or rule 8.104(a)(2) (180 days from
  entry)?
- Were there any tolling motions (new trial, JNOV,
  reconsideration, vacate)? Were they timely?
- Was the NOA premature (filed before the order was
  final)? If so, does the premature-notice doctrine save it?

### Preservation / Forfeiture (Per Issue)
For EACH issue raised on appeal:
- Was the issue raised below? Where in the record?
- Was the objection specific enough to preserve the issue?
- Was there a motion (e.g., directed verdict, JNOV, new
  trial) that preserved it?
- Any invited error problems?
- Any forfeiture by inconsistent position?
- If not preserved: is there a basis to argue the issue
  anyway (pure legal question, futility, forfeiture vs.
  waiver distinction, judicial economy)?

### Standing and Mootness
- Does the appellant have standing?
- Has anything changed during the appeal that might render
  the case moot?
- If moot: is there an exception (capable of repetition yet
  evading review, voluntary cessation, collateral
  consequences)?

### Sua Sponte Concerns
- Are there issues the court might raise on its own that
  neither party briefed?
- Jurisdiction (always fair game)
- Standing
- Compliance with appellate rules (page limits, extensions,
  record designation completeness)

## Section 7: Zinger Anticipation

These are the "gotcha" questions — things a curious or hostile
judge might raise that go beyond what the briefs address.

### Uncited Authority
- Are there cases or statutes that neither side cited that
  the panel might have found independently?
- Any recent opinions (published since briefing closed)
  that bear on the issues?
- Any amicus positions or pending legislation?

### Internal Tensions
- Are there internal tensions or inconsistencies in our
  own position?
- Did we take a position at trial that is hard to reconcile
  with our position on appeal?
- Does our argument on Issue A undermine our argument on
  Issue B?

### Policy Consequences
- What are the practical consequences of the rule we're
  asking the court to adopt?
- Floodgates — would this open the door to a wave of
  similar claims?
- Administrability — is the rule workable for trial courts?
- Perverse incentives — does our rule create bad incentives
  for future litigants?
- Institutional concerns — does our position require the
  court to second-guess a coordinate branch?

### Adverse Record Facts
- Are there facts in the record that cut against us that
  the panel might have noticed?
- Any admissions, stipulations, or concessions in the
  record that weaken our position?
- Any evidence we wish wasn't there?

### Workability Questions
- How would our proposed rule apply in hypothetical
  variations of the facts?
- Can we articulate a principled limiting principle?
- Would a judge considering "what about this scenario?"
  find our rule produces sensible results?

---

## Section 8: Concessions & Landmines

### Safe Concessions
- What can we safely concede — and how should we frame the
  concession so it actually strengthens our position?
- "Even assuming [X], the result is the same because [Y]"
  formulations

### Landmines
- What topics should we avoid raising or dwelling on?
- Adverse authority we should not invite the court to
  focus on
- Facts that look worse the more attention they get
- Standard-of-review traps (e.g., inviting de novo review
  of a discretionary decision)

### Safe Routes
- For each landmine, a brief recommended approach:
  acknowledge and pivot, distinguish, or concede and cabin

---

## Section 9: Close-Out Toolkit

### Three Likely Opening Questions
Based on the full analysis, predict the three questions the
presiding justice is most likely to open with. Consider:
- The most obvious tension in the case
- The procedural posture
- The panel's known interests (from Phase A)

### Three Crisp Answers
One-sentence answers to each opening question. These should
be tight enough to deliver in 10–15 seconds each.

### 20-Second Closing Statement
Links the standard of review, the clean rule we want, and
why the relief is correct. This is the final statement
if the court gives the attorney a chance to close.
